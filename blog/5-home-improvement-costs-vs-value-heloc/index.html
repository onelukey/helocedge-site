Which Home Improvements Are Worth Financing? A Cost vs. Value Guide

9 min read
Mike Lucas — Founder

Not every renovation adds value in proportion to what it costs. Before deciding how to fund a project — with a HELOC, a personal loan, or savings — it's worth stepping back and asking whether the project is worth financing at all. Some upgrades pay for themselves, or come close to it, at resale. Others are purely lifestyle spending that happen to involve a contractor. Both can be reasonable choices, but they call for different funding decisions.

A quick way to think about it

Projects that improve function, efficiency, and curb appeal (roofing, siding, garage doors, minor kitchen updates) tend to recoup a higher share of their cost at resale than large, highly personalized projects (pools, high-end primary suite additions, luxury kitchen overhauls).

Typical cost ranges by project

Actual costs vary significantly by region, home size, and finish level, but the ranges below reflect what's commonly reported for mid-range projects across the US. Use them as a starting point for your own budgeting, not a quote.

Project Typical cost range Value recovery at resale
Minor kitchen remodel $15,000 – $30,000 Typically high
Full kitchen remodel $30,000 – $75,000+ Moderate
Bathroom remodel $10,000 – $25,000 Typically high
Roof replacement $9,000 – $20,000 Typically high
HVAC replacement $6,000 – $15,000 Typically high
Primary suite addition $50,000 – $150,000+ Typically low-moderate
Deck or patio $8,000 – $25,000 Typically high
Swimming pool $40,000 – $90,000+ Typically low
Solar panel installation $15,000 – $30,000 Moderate, market-dependent

Figures are general US mid-range estimates for planning purposes only and will vary by location, materials, and labor costs. Get itemized quotes from licensed local contractors before budgeting a project.

Resale value isn't the only reason to renovate

If you plan to stay in your home long-term, a project's day-to-day value to your household — comfort, safety, energy savings, or simply enjoying the space — can matter more than resale recovery. The cost-vs-value framing is most useful if a sale is realistically on the horizon.

Why this matters for how you fund the project

Once you've weighed a project's cost against its likely value, the funding decision tends to fall into one of a few patterns:

  • High-value, essential projects (roof, HVAC, siding) are strong candidates for HELOC financing, since they protect or increase your home's value and often need to happen on a timeline outside your control.
  • Phased or multi-room renovations — say, a kitchen this year and bathrooms next — suit a HELOC particularly well, because you can draw only what each phase needs rather than borrowing the full projected cost up front.
  • Large lifestyle projects with lower resale recovery (pools, luxury additions) are still reasonable to finance if they genuinely improve your quality of life, but it's worth being more conservative about how much you borrow and over what term, since you're less likely to recover the cost if you sell soon after.
Why a HELOC suits phased renovations specifically

A home equity loan or personal loan gives you a fixed lump sum on day one, whether or not you need it all immediately. A HELOC's draw period lets you borrow in stages as each phase of a multi-project renovation gets underway — so you're not paying interest on funds sitting unused while you wait for the next contractor.

Working out what you can afford to finance

Before committing to a project list, it helps to see two numbers side by side: how much equity you could realistically draw against, and what the monthly payment would look like once the draw period ends and repayment begins.
