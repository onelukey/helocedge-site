HELOC vs. Personal Loan vs. Credit Card: How to Pay for a Home Improvement Project

8 min read
Mike Lucas — Founder

Once you've decided a renovation is worth doing, the next question is how to pay for it. Most homeowners end up choosing between three options: a HELOC, a personal loan, or a credit card. Each works differently, and the right choice depends less on which is "best" overall and more on your project size, timeline, and how much certainty you want over your payments.

The three options at a glance

Feature HELOC Personal loan Credit card
Typical interest rate Varies — secured by home Varies — usually unsecured Varies — usually unsecured
Rate type Usually variable Usually fixed Usually variable
Access to funds Revolving — draw as needed One-time lump sum Revolving
Collateral required Yes — your home No No
Approval speed Slower — appraisal & underwriting Often fast Fast, if already have a card
Potential use to compare Staged borrowing needs Fixed-amount borrowing Revolving purchases
Collateral is the key trade-off

A HELOC is secured by your home, which may affect its pricing compared with unsecured credit. But rates, fees and total costs vary by offer. Missing required HELOC payments can put your home at risk of foreclosure. Compare both borrowing costs and collateral risk, rather than assuming any one product is cheaper.

HELOC: revolving access to funds

A HELOC generally allows borrowing in stages during a draw period, which can be relevant when renovation expenses arrive at different times. Interest is generally calculated on the amount drawn, but required payments may include principal or fees depending on the agreement. A variable rate, later repayment-period changes and the risk to your home are important trade-offs.

The trade-off is time and structure: opening a HELOC typically involves a home appraisal and a underwriting process that takes longer than a personal loan or credit card application, and most HELOCs carry a variable rate, meaning your payment can change over the life of the loan.

Personal loan: a fixed amount up front

A personal loan generally provides a fixed amount up front. Some offer fixed rates and scheduled payments, which can make repayment amounts easier to estimate. Terms and approval times vary, and unsecured personal loans generally do not use your home as collateral.

Rates, origination fees, repayment terms and early-repayment provisions vary. Interest costs depend on the amount outstanding and the loan agreement; receiving a lump sum may mean paying interest before every renovation expense occurs.

Credit card: revolving unsecured credit

Credit cards allow revolving purchases, such as materials and fixtures, within an available limit. Some cards offer introductory 0% APR periods, but promotional terms, fees and the rate that applies afterward need careful checking. Deferred-interest promotions can work differently from a true 0% introductory APR.

The risk with using a card for a full renovation

Carrying a card balance after a promotion ends can increase interest costs substantially. Actual costs depend on the card's APR, fees, balance and repayment schedule, so comparing written terms and total repayment estimates is more useful than assuming one borrowing type is always most expensive.

Questions to use when comparing options

  • Project over roughly $20,000, or spread across phases? Compare how staged draws, changing rates, fees and home-collateral risk would affect the project.
  • Project with a firm, known cost and you want a fixed payment? Compare lump-sum funding and any fixed-payment terms with the other options.
  • Small purchases or a short-term gap? Compare promotional terms, credit limits, fees and the cost of carrying a balance.

Whichever option you're leaning toward, it's worth running the actual numbers before deciding. Takeaway

There is no universal winner among these products. Compare written offers, repayment obligations, total costs, flexibility and the consequences of missed payments. In particular, a HELOC puts the home at risk, while unsecured borrowing has different costs and consequences. For independent information, see the CFPB's credit-card explanations and its HELOC guide.
