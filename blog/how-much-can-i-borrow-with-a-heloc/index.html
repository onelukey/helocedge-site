How Much Can I Reasonably Borrow With a HELOC?

5 min read Mike Lucas

When homeowners decide to open a Home Equity Line of Credit, the very first question they ask is almost always: "How much cash can I actually get out of my house?"

Having $100,000 in home equity does not necessarily mean a lender will offer a $100,000 HELOC. Lenders set their own borrowing limits and consider other financial factors before making an offer.

One useful starting point for estimating potential borrowing room is Combined Loan-to-Value (CLTV).

Understanding CLTV Limits: An 80% Example

Some lenders use CLTV limits around 80% to 85%, but policies vary by lender, loan product and borrower. These percentages are examples, not universal limits.

CLTV compares the total debt secured against a home, including the proposed HELOC credit limit and any existing mortgage or other liens, with the home's estimated value. The lender's chosen limit determines the borrowing room available under this particular calculation.

How to Estimate Borrowing Room (Step-by-Step)

Here's a hypothetical example using an assumed 80% CLTV limit. It illustrates the arithmetic, not a lender's underwriting decision:

Item Amount
Estimated current home value $400,000
Current remaining mortgage balance $250,000
Assumed CLTV limit for this example 80%
Step 1: Home value × CLTV cap ($400,000 × 0.80) $320,000
Step 2: Subtract existing secured debt ($320,000 − $250,000) $70,000

Illustrative borrowing room under these assumptions: $70,000. Any additional liens would reduce this figure; an actual lender may offer a different amount or decline an application.

Other Factors That Can Affect a Lender's Decision

A CLTV estimate does not establish eligibility. Lenders may also consider factors such as income, existing debt, credit history, property valuation and their own lending policies:

  • Debt-to-Income (DTI) Ratio: Debt-to-income ratio compares recurring monthly debt obligations with gross monthly income. How a lender calculates it and what ratio it accepts can vary.
  • Credit Score: Credit history and credit scores may affect eligibility and pricing, but there is no single score that guarantees approval or a particular interest rate.
Your home value estimate matters

An overstated home value can make an estimate look more generous than a lender's assessment. Use a reasonable, supportable estimate; lenders may use appraisals, automated valuations or other methods to assess a property's value.

Conclusion

Comparing a reasonable home-value estimate with existing secured debt and a hypothetical CLTV limit can help you understand the numbers before speaking with a lender. Actual offers depend on lender policies and individual circumstances. For an independent overview of HELOC features and risks, see the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's HELOC guide.
